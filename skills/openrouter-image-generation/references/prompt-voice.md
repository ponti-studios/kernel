# Writing image prompts in positive voice

## The rule and its mechanism

State what IS present. Never name a thing you want absent — not with "no", not
with "never", not as "not wearing", not as a negation in a negative list.

Image conditioning does not encode negation as a logical operator. Prompt tokens
are attended to as content: "no dog" carries _dog_. Instruction-tuned image
models can partially obey a prohibition, but attention is still allocated to the
named thing, so a negative raises its probability even when it is partly
honoured. This is why a brief can specify a constraint five times and still get
the opposite, and why _repeating_ a prohibition makes it worse rather than
clearer.

Consequence for prompt architecture: constraints belong in the clause that
already describes the thing positively, and absences are expressed by leaving
them out.

## The anti-pattern to recognise

**Multiple entities in one frame, each told what the others wear.** A prompt
that says:

```
STAGING — Akane, in the deep-red wrap dress; never the black cardigan, white
  t-shirt and black cargo trousers; never the plain black t-shirt and black jeans
STAGING — Lucy, in the plain black t-shirt and black jeans; never the
  deep-red wrap dress
STAGING — Wyatt, in the black cardigan, white t-shirt and black cargo trousers;
  never the deep-red wrap dress
```

is not a wardrobe lock. It tokenises every garment and attaches all of them to
all three characters, so a garment can arrive on the wrong person — the "swap"
is generated by the sentence meant to prevent it. The longer the cast, the worse
it gets: N characters produce N×(N-1) forbidden-garment mentions.

Write it as ownership instead, naming only the owner's own garments:

```
STAGING — Akane, wearing the deep-red wrap dress and nothing else: …
STAGING — Lucy, wearing the plain black t-shirt and black jeans and nothing else: …
```

plus one global line that states distinctness without enumerating anyone else's
clothes: _"each named character wears exactly the outfit stated for them, and
nothing else; the outfits in this frame are distinct in colour and silhouette."_

Recognise the shape anywhere it appears: a **list of exclusions attached to the
wrong subject** is worse than no clause at all.

## Converting a negative to a positive

| Negative                                                               | Positive replacement                                                                                                   |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| never transparent, never floating                                      | the robe is dense opaque cloth                                                                                         |
| no tattoos on his hands                                                | his hands are clean bare skin                                                                                          |
| no readable text anywhere                                              | every surface is plain and unlettered — the screen shows soft abstract light, signs and cups are plain coloured shapes |
| two round holes … no ventilation dots, no perforations, no other holes | one smooth black surface with two round holes at the eyes                                                              |
| nobody looks at the camera                                             | every gaze is fixed on the subject _(and delete the negative)_                                                         |
| no <name> in this frame                                                | _(delete — absence by omission)_                                                                                       |
| no blood, gore, wounds or weapons                                      | clean costumes and harmless theatrical props; nobody is hurt                                                           |
| never a baby or chibi build                                            | adult construction throughout, five heads tall                                                                         |

Two rules for the conversion itself:

- **A prohibition with a positive half keeps the positive half.** "Wyatt wears
  only his cardigan — never the Passenger's greatcoat" becomes "Wyatt wears only
  his cardigan"; the tail is the part that names another character's clothing.
- **A constraint you cannot rephrase positively is reported, not deleted.**
  Dropping it silently loses information the author intended. Keep it, flag it,
  and let the author rewrite it — a compile-time report is the right place for
  that, not a quiet omission.

## The one exception

Text suppression is conventional and effective: _"no readable text, no
lettering, no captions, no logos, no watermark"_. The training signal against
generated lettering is unusually strong, so the negative earns its place here.
Keep it to that one clause; a prompt that negates text, logos, wordmarks,
captions, signage and badges across three clauses is back to paying the
attention tax.

## Auditing a prompt (or a template that emits one)

Count, do not eyeball:

```bash
# negation words per 1k characters — flag anything above ~3
wc -c < prompt.txt
 grep -oiE '\b(no|never|not|none|without|nothing|nobody|nowhere)\b' prompt.txt | wc -l

# the specific anti-pattern
 grep -n 'never the ' prompt.txt
```

For a compiler or template that generates prompts, make this a **check in the
harness** rather than a habit: the generated output must contain no
"never the" garment enumeration and must stay under a negation budget. A rule
enforced only by memory regresses the next time someone edits the template.

### Where negation hides in an assembled prompt

A generated prompt draws text from more places than the prose you can see, and
cleaning the obvious ones leaves most of the negation in place. Walking the
sources in this order is what finds it — the last four are the ones that went
unnoticed through two passes:

| Source                                                                                    | Enters as                                                                                      |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| canon / character blocks                                                                  | extracted verbatim per entity                                                                  |
| shared blocks in the registry or config                                                   | appended last to every prompt                                                                  |
| structured records: per-item outfit/attribute descriptions                                | the attribute clause and ownership lines                                                       |
| per-frame constraint lists                                                                | the content/negatives clause                                                                   |
| **nested object fields** (prop `description`, `state`)                                    | their own clauses — skipped by a scan of top-level prose fields                                |
| **free prose fields** (`moment`, `environment`, `lighting`, cast `action`, `gaze_target`) | their own clauses and the staging line                                                         |
| **the generator's own emitted text**                                                      | staging, wardrobe, scale, ownership lines — feels exempt because you are editing it; it is not |

Measured on a real pipeline: cleaning the canon blocks took them to zero while
every compiled frame still carried ~45 negation words. Bucket hits by the clause
they land in and re-measure each pass; a source-level tally ("the canon is at
zero") says nothing about the compiled output. One shared block the generator
tacked onto every prompt was itself violating the rule the generator documents.

### When a consumer quotes the text by exact string

If the pipeline supports per-frame overrides that delete or replace a sentence
by matching it, reflowing the source prose breaks them: the words are identical
but the whitespace is not, and the failure surfaces as a _missing sentence_
rather than a wrong render. Match such overrides whitespace-tolerantly, keep the
fixture suite as the gate after every source edit, and when a missing-sentence
refusal appears, treat the spec's quote as stale rather than loosening the check.

A measured effect of converting a real 4-character frame: the garment
enumeration clause carried six forbidden-garment mentions, and the render it
produced lost one character's top entirely while risking a swap on the others;
with the clause replaced by ownership-plus-distinctness, every specified garment
rendered, on the correct person. Treat the direction as the lesson and the
single sample as an illustration, not proof.

## Verifying a voice change (it is not automatically an improvement)

A three-frame, two-arm test of a full positive-voice conversion came back
**mixed**, which is the honest expectation for this kind of change:

- One property improved: a wide-mesh prop that used to hang through the figure
  bunched over it once its support was rewritten.
- One property **regressed**: a robe asserted as _"dense opaque cloth"_ rendered
  translucent, where the old _"never transparent"_ had held. Opacity may be a
  second case — alongside text suppression — where an explicit prohibition
  outperforms a positive assertion, so check properties individually rather than
  assuming the conversion is neutral.
- The positive arm also gained objects the control arm did not have (an extra
  prop, an invented tool).
- The failure the rewrite was meant to prevent appeared in **neither** arm — so
  that arm was not failing and nothing was being fixed there. A conversion can be
  correct as a matter of principle and still have no measurable effect on a given
  frame; say so rather than implying a quality win.

Rules that follow:

- **One sample per arm proves nothing.** To claim a voice change improved output,
  render at least five samples per arm on the frame with the strongest prior
  failure and count incidents — extra objects, lost properties, wrong garment
  attributions. Anything less cannot separate signal from ordinary render
  variance, and the variance is large enough to reverse the apparent ranking
  between runs.
- **Check the property, not the prompt.** A rewrite that removes negation is
  mechanically verifiable (count the words, diff the clauses); a rewrite that
  _improves renders_ is an empirical claim needing the sampling above. Report
  the two separately.
- **Judge both arms in one look** by compositing them side by side and asking a
  single question about each panel:

  ```bash
  ffmpeg -y -i legacy.png -i positive.png -filter_complex hstack=inputs=2 pair.png
  ```

  Then state the order in the question ("LEFT = legacy, RIGHT = positive"). It
  halves the vision calls and forces a like-for-like answer; `magick montage
  -label` needs a font that may not be installed, so an unlabelled stack plus a
  stated order is the more reliable route.

## Relationship to other rules

- Geometry (what holds a prop up, what occludes what) is a separate discipline —
  see `prompt-geometry.md`. Positive voice is not a substitute for describing
  support and contact: state the physical arrangement that forces the look,
  rather than prohibiting the look you do not want.
- Where a system compiles prompts from structured specs, put the voice rule in
  the **compiler**, not in the specs, so every frame inherits it and the spec
  author does not have to remember.
