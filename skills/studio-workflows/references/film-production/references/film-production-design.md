# Film Production Design

_The environment is the character's psychology made physical._

This document covers the world-building craft of Ponti Studios film work: how environments define character, when to use plannametric composition, and the rules for wardrobe and props. It assumes the philosophy established in [[film-philosophy]].

For on-set capture, see [[film-capture]]. For post-production, see [[film-editing]] and [[film-sound]].

---

## Environment Defines Character

The character is defined by their environment. (_Fincher_)

- A messy apartment is not a set decoration choice — it is a psychological document. The character did not arrive in this space by accident. Every object, every stain, every arrangement tells us who they are.
- We do not design "nice" locations. We design **revealing** locations. The right location exposes something about the character that dialogue would overstate.
- When a character enters a space, the audience should learn something about them from how they relate to it — do they belong? Do they arrange? Do they ignore?

---

## Plannametric Composition (Anderson's Method)

Anderson keeps backgrounds as two-dimensional planes relative to the character. Characters occupy and move through the background rather than being immersed in deep space.

We use this technique when:

- We need graphic clarity (a busy background would distract from the character's action)
- The scene's tone is formal, constructed, or heightened
- The character is navigating a world that is itself artificial or constrained

We avoid it when:

- We need immersion and atmosphere
- The scene requires the audience to feel the texture of a real space
- The character is lost in an environment they do not control

---

## Wardrobe and Props

- Wardrobe is character information, not aesthetic preference. If a character's clothes do not tell us something about their profession, class, psychology, or transformation arc, they are wrong.
- Props must earn their place in the frame. A prop that is noticed but never used is a lie. Every object in frame must either serve the narrative or be removed.
