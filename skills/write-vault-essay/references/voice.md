# Longform Voice Contract

Your longform is not a longer Monotone piece. It is Monotone's *ethics* (essential, honest, still, cut every word that doesn't work, white space as structure) extended with New Yorker *instruments* (wit, erudition, extended metaphor, concrete financial math, scene-setting).

## What Is Preserved (from ponti voice canon) — MUST

- **Essential, honest, still.** Say the hard thing plainly and get out. Last line is a strike, not a summary.
- **Kanso:** every word earns its place. If removing a word changes nothing, delete it.
- **Ma:** a single-sentence paragraph alone is a landing, not an accident.
- **Shibui:** no typographic inflation. If a sentence needs bold to land, rewrite it.
- **Wabi-sabi:** honesty about holes. The most powerful paragraph is often the most honest one.
- **Four rules:** open on strike, say hard thing plainly, no filler/hedges, in-done-gone.
- **Never:** exclamation, emoji, "In recent years...", explaining the metaphor, ending with question/CTA, academic transitions (thus/therefore/however/furthermore), uniform paragraphs.

## What Is Adapted (for longform) — MUST

**Length & pacing:**
- Monotone: 500w is ideal, 900 is failure, rhythm over 5 paras.
- Longform: 1000w is ideal, rhythm over ~12-15 paras + 3 section breaks. Still at least two single-sentence landings, no two adjacent same length — but now across sections.

**Structure:**
- Monotone: Strike / Setup / Deepening / Obstacle / Exit — no headers, the structure disappears.
- Longform: Strike / Setup / Deepening / **Arithmetic** / Obstacle / Exit — with `###` headers as signposts. Headers are not decoration; they are `Ma` — they give the eye a place to rest before a dense proof. Example from approved essay: `### The $15-Million Cameo`, `### The Mid-Budget Alibi`, `### The Arithmetic of Fragmentation`.

**Evidence:**
- Monotone: never cite a study or statistic — argument from observation only.
- Longform: **statistics required** for economics/media essays. Keep source fidelity: $50M budget, $92M gross, $125M breakeven, $2.5B content spend, $35-50 ARPU. Never invent. If source was spitballing ("$30-50"), keep the conservative or mark TBD.

**Typography:**
- Monotone: never italics or bold.
- Longform: **italics allowed** ~1 word per 250w, only to mark the metaphor's shibboleth or film/book titles: *wedding*, *bridal*, *wedding tax*, *Barbie*. Never bold for emphasis.

**Tone:**
- Monotone: quiet, precise, absent of hype. "A smart person thinking in public."
- Longform: same quiet precision **plus** New Yorker dry wit and scene-setting: the caterer phone call, the hallway in Burbank, the ballroom on a Tuesday night. Wit is not decoration — it is the invitation. Erudition is restrained: one historical reference, one cultural anchor, never a pile.

## New Yorker Instruments — the expressive layer (MUST for longform)

The rules above keep an essay from being bad. These make it good. Every approved longform essay deploys all seven. A piece that passes the checker but uses none of these is a well-structured brief, not an essay.

1. **Scene before claim.** The essay opens in a specific moment a reader can picture — a phone call, a room, a number on a screen. The thesis is implied by the scene, never stated before it. (Exemplar: the caterer call — the reader hears the phone, sees the chicken, hears the word *wedding*.) Rule of thumb: if the first paragraph could run as a news brief, it is not expressive enough to open an essay.
2. **A lived metaphor.** The metaphor is not announced; it is staged. You walk through the venue, the ballroom, the kitchen. It recurs with a new physical detail in every section and lands, physically, in the final line.
3. **Dry wit by specificity.** At least one moment where precision, not a punchline, produces the smile: a $15 million cake that says *Prestige* in buttercream; a man paid to shovel coal into a locomotive that runs on oil. Wit is the writer's amusement at how exactly the world works. A pun or a joke is the failure mode; never do either.
4. **Erudition worn lightly.** Exactly one unexpected widening reference — a roulette dealer's wrist, a physicist's clarity, Sowell — that the argument has earned. Not a pile, never a name-drop.
5. **Breath rhythm.** Long sentences that accumulate clauses (18-40 words), broken by 3-8 word strikes. The eye should breathe with the argument. The paragraph-rhythm rule governs length between paragraphs; this governs length inside them. Two long sentences, then a strike, is the house rhythm.
6. **Staged arithmetic.** Numbers are a scene, not a citation: friendly numbers first, then leakage, then the landing ("not $5 or $15, but north of $35 to $50 *for one catalog*"). The math should feel walked-through, not reported.
7. **The writer present.** The essay has an attitude — amused, calm, exact — and a judgment the reader can feel without being told. It is a sensibility, not a thesis with evidence.

Test: read the first paragraph aloud. If it sounds like a person talking to you in a room — not a brief, not a headline — you have the instruments.

## Checklist Before Shipping Longform

- Does the first line make you argue or pause?
- Can you state the claim in one sentence and the metaphor in two words?
- Are two single-sentence landings present and not adjacent?
- Are headers 2-4 and doing work (not decorating)?
- Are numbers traceable to source?
- Does italics appear sparingly (≤1 per ~250w) and only for the metaphor or titles?
- Does last line land without explaining?
- **Does it open on a scene, not a thesis?** (Instrument 1)
- **Is the metaphor lived — new physical detail in every section, landed in the final line?** (Instrument 2)
- **Is there one moment of dry wit from precision, never a pun?** (Instrument 3)
- **Is there exactly one widening reference, earned?** (Instrument 4)
- **Do long clause-stacked sentences break to short strikes inside paragraphs?** (Instrument 5)
- **Is the math staged as walked-through proof, not a citation?** (Instrument 6)

If any is no, cut or rewrite before shipping.
