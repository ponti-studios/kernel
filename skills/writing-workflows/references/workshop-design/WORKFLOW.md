# Workshop Design

> Invoke with: the workshop to design.

## Definition of done

- outcome, participants, decisions, and format are explicit
- every agenda block has a purpose and a tangible output
- the agenda uses the closest workshop playbook
- unresolved decisions and risks are named, not buried

Design workshops that produce decisions and artifacts, not discussion. A
workshop earns the room only if it must end with a decision made or an
artifact built — if it needs neither, it is a meeting, and this skill refuses
the job. The method below is synthesized from the GV Design Sprint
methodology (map, sketch, decide, prototype, test) and standard facilitation
practice: diverge before converging, timebox everything, and put the
decision-maker in the room.

## Method

### 1. Frame the workshop

Extract these from the request; ask when they are missing and stop there,
because format and agenda follow from them:

- **Outcome** — the single decision to make or artifact to build.
- **Non-goals** — what this session will explicitly not settle.
- **Format** — in-person, remote, or hybrid; duration available.
- **Constraints** — missing authority, research, or capacity that bounds
  what can be promised.

Do not promise outcomes that require a missing decider, missing research, or
missing implementation capacity.

### 2. Staff the room

- **Size:** 5–8 people optimal, 3–15 workable. Larger groups split into
  breakouts, never into audiences.
- **Decider:** one person with authority over the outcome. Mandatory for
  every decision block; ties break by supervote, not consensus.
- **Facilitator:** neutral owner of time, energy, and process. Contributes
  no content opinions.
- **Scribe:** captures outputs and the decision log in real time.
- **Doers and experts:** the people who will build on the outcome, plus
  domain experts for input blocks only.
- No spectators. No laptops or phones except the scribe's and the
  prototyping station's.
- **Match exercises to temperaments.** Shy domain experts do badly with
  improv games — give them brainwriting instead. A known dominator gets
  silent-generation exercises that equalize voices, or no invite.
- **Never facilitate alone.** Recruit a co-facilitator to run sections and
  a documenter to capture photos, quotes, and artifacts for the recap.

### 3. Assign pre-work

Pre-work covers whatever the session depends on: research, stakeholder
context, analytics, design files, technical constraints, decider input. Every
pre-work item names an owner and a deadline. If critical pre-work is missing
on the day, reschedule — do not improvise the foundation live.

Bake the whole plan into slides or a whiteboard before the day: a section
per activity, including breaks and recap sections, so the plan lives outside
the facilitator's head. Show up early to test A/V, set out materials, and
check the room for noise, temperature, and wall space.

### 4. Build the agenda

Plan backwards from the outcomes: list what each goal needs, turn each need
into an activity, then assign time estimates with buffer — activities always
run long, and transitions after breaks eat minutes. Never overstuff the day.

- **Open with a warm-up.** Participants arrive distracted from other work;
  jumping cold into brainstorming burns time and momentum. Start with a
  short, fun, unrelated exercise — an improv game (alphabet, sound ball,
  three things, word-at-a-time proverb, with virtual variants) or a quick
  sketch task — and explain its purpose up front to disarm the skeptics.
- **Diverge, then converge, per block.** Generate options silently and
  individually first (notes, sketches, HMWs); evaluate and rank together
  after. Silent generation beats brainstorming out loud.
- **Run brainstorms under IDEO's rules:** defer judgment, encourage wild
  ideas, build on the ideas of others, stay focused on the topic, one
  conversation at a time, be visual, go for quantity. Quantity first —
  the "bad" and unfeasible ideas get pared later, and their fragments
  regularly survive into the winner. Seed the room with two or three
  example ideas and constraint prompts ("what if we had a week / a
  million dollars?") for stuck groups.
- **Share out loud, then narrow.** Every sketch goes on the wall or board
  where all ideas are equally visible; each author walks through theirs
  with buffer for discussion. Narrow with dot-voting so every voice counts
  equally, and give every surviving idea a named author for follow-up
  questions.
- **Every block has a purpose and a tangible output.** Prefer making,
  mapping, ranking, deciding, and assigning ownership over discussion-only
  blocks. A block that produces only talk gets cut or converted.
- **Timebox with a light touch.** Announce the time up front, keep one eye
  on the clock, hold the box — but skip the visible countdown timer, which
  creates pressure that kills generative thinking.
- **Manage energy.** Hard thinking in the morning; breaks at least every
  90 minutes with water and snacks; alternate solo, pair, and group modes.
- **Surface disagreement early.** Treat it as signal. Design exercises that
  force differences onto the wall (heat-map voting, speed critique) before
  converging, never after.
- **Park off-topic items visibly.** A parking lot the room can see keeps
  focus without losing ideas; address leftovers after the session.
- **Avoid hybrid if possible.** Mixed in-person/remote rooms split the
  group into first- and second-class voices. Go all in-person or all
  remote; when mixed is unavoidable, make the virtual whiteboard the one
  shared canvas everyone works on. Remote sessions otherwise need cameras
  on for decision blocks, shorter blocks than in-person, and explicit
  check-ins at each transition.

### 5. Run the close

End with the decision log (decision, owner, date), deliverables with owners,
and a dated follow-up. Close the last five minutes on next steps and live
feedback on the session itself, while memory is sharp. Name unresolved
decisions and risks clearly — an unresolved item written down beats a false
consensus every time. Send a recap to participants and stakeholders afterward
with photos and artifacts from the room; it sustains momentum and carries
the decisions to people who were not there.

## Output structure

Default to this order:

1. Workshop recommendation (format and why it fits)
2. Goals and non-goals
3. Participants and roles
4. Pre-work (item, owner, deadline)
5. Agenda with timeboxes (block, duration, purpose, output, materials)
6. Facilitation guide (per-block notes: diverge/converge mode, decision rule, traps)
7. Deliverables with owners
8. Follow-up plan plus named unresolved decisions and risks

For client-facing copy, keep it polished and concise and follow the voice in
[canon-brand-copy.md](references/canon-brand-copy.md). For internal facilitation, make it practical
and detailed enough that a different facilitator could run the session cold.

## Quality bar

- [ ] The session ends in a decision or an artifact — verified, not assumed
- [ ] Every agenda block states its purpose and its tangible output
- [ ] The Decider is present for every decision block
- [ ] The agenda opens with a warm-up unrelated to the project
- [ ] Diverge precedes converge in every generative block
- [ ] Brainstorms run under posted rules with quantity over quality
- [ ] Share-out gives every idea equal wall space, dot-voting, and a named author
- [ ] Timeboxes hold without a visible countdown pressuring the room
- [ ] Disagreement is surfaced by exercise design, early, not by debate late
- [ ] Pre-work items have owners and deadlines; gaps force reschedule
- [ ] No outcome is promised on missing authority, research, or capacity
- [ ] Unresolved decisions and risks are written down explicitly
- [ ] Recap with photos and artifacts goes to participants and stakeholders
