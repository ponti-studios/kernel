# Film Capture

_What happens on set._

This document covers the practical craft of capturing footage: how we compose frames, use color, and move the camera. It assumes the philosophy established in [[film-philosophy]].

For world-building, see [[film-production-design]]. For post-production, see [[film-editing]] and [[film-sound]].

---

## The Frame

### Composition Principles

1. **Function before decoration.** Every element in the frame must serve the story being told at that exact moment. If a prop, a color, or a background element does not communicate something about the narrative or character, it is removed.

2. **The eye must know where to land.** Use composition, color contrast, or focus to direct attention. The audience should never search for what matters in a frame.

3. **Symmetry is a tool, not a rule.** Anderson uses symmetry to create constructed worlds where every element feels placed. We use symmetry when the world is ordered, and break symmetry when the world is unstable. The decision is intentional, not habitual.

4. **Depth is earned.** Flat compositions (plannametric) create graphic clarity. Deep compositions create immersion. Choose based on what the scene needs. Do not default to shallow depth-of-field because it looks "cinematic."

5. **The frame edge is a boundary, not a constraint.** What is outside the frame matters as much as what is inside. A character looking off-screen creates tension. A sound from an unseen source creates dread. Use what is absent.

### Color

- **Color is information.** A contrasting color within an otherwise unified palette tells the audience what to focus on. This is Fincher's method — desaturated environments with a single warm accent (blood, a lamp, a character's hair) that draws the eye before the edit decides where to look.
- **Palette before grade.** We choose production colors (wardrobe, location, light) before we consider post-production grading. Color in the world is stronger than color added in the timeline.
- **We do not use teal-and-orange.** It is the default because it works on flesh tones, and the default is precisely what we refuse.
- **Monochrome is a discipline.** If we shoot black and white, it is because the story cannot tolerate the distraction of color — not because it looks timeless.

**Prescription:**

- Establish a dominant palette for each project or series.
- Use one accent color per scene for narrative emphasis.
- Grade in service of mood, not correction. Let the source material's color temperature be visible.

## Camera

### Stillness

The locked-off shot is our foundation. We place the camera and let the world move within it. This achieves three things:

1. **Authority.** The frame says: what you are about to see is worth watching without tricks.
2. **Subjective distance.** A still camera allows the audience to observe rather than be pushed. They decide where to look.
3. **Emphasis on movement.** When the camera does move, it registers. The movement carries weight because stillness is the baseline.

### Movement

When we move the camera, we follow principles:

- **If the character moves, we move.** The camera is tethered to the character's physical experience. A dolly follow, a pan, a track — these are in service of staying with the character as they navigate space.
- **Movement defines mental state.** Erratic movement signals instability. Smooth movement signals control. The camera becomes a proxy for the character's psychological condition.
- **We move for one reason only.** Before each move, ask: does this movement serve the story? If the answer requires more than one sentence, lock the camera off.
- **No unmotivated zooms.** A zoom is a decision. It selects what matters. It must be timed to a realization, a reveal, or a shift in understanding.

### Compass Point Editing (Anderson's Method)

Camera pans in 90-degree increments. The camera faces one direction, then rotates exactly 90 degrees to face another. The result is a constructed, formal quality — the audience feels the geometry of the space.

We use this technique when:

- The spatial relationships between characters matter to the scene
- We want the audience to feel the constructed nature of the frame (not all scenes require invisible craft)
- The scene benefits from graphic clarity over naturalistic immersion

### Camera as Proxy for Consciousness

**Fincher's method:** The camera follows the character's train of thought. If the character notices something, we see what they see. If the character moves through a space, we follow at their pace. The edit follows their attention.

We adopt this as a core principle: **the camera is not objective. It is subjective — tethered to a character's experience.** This applies to:

- Coverage: what we show (and do not show) is determined by whose story this moment belongs to.
- POV: we use subjective shots not as gimmicks but as structural tools — the audience should inhabit a perspective, not just observe it.
- Attention: the camera drifts to what the character would notice. A detail in the background, a person entering the room, an object on a desk.
